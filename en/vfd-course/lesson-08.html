Braking and stopping

Starting is easy; stopping is what causes trouble. When you stop a heavy load fast, the motor turns into a generator and sends the energy back into the drive. Try it yourself: you will cause an OV fault, then fix it.

1How does the motor stop?

The two simplest ways: ramp to stop, where the drive lowers the frequency over the decel time, which suits conveyors and pumps. And coast to stop, where the drive cuts its output and lets the motor run down, used for heavy fans.

If you want the shaft held at the end of the stop you use DC injection: the drive feeds DC into the windings and holds the shaft.

Loads that send back a lot of energy when stopping have two answers: a braking resistor that burns the returned energy (hoists and centrifuges), or regen / AFE that returns it to the mains (lifts).

2Why does OV trip while the motor is stopping?

If decel is faster than the load can stop, the load drives the motor, so the motor becomes a generator and sends energy back into the drive. That energy raises the DC bus voltage, and at about 800 V DC the drive trips on OV.

A feature called the bus regulator stretches the decel by itself so the trip does not happen. But if you fit a braking resistor, turn it off so the resistor can work.

3Try it: stop a heavy flywheel

Shorten the decel and watch the DC bus voltage, then try again with the resistor.

Braking simulator

4How do you fit a braking resistor correctly?

Choose the resistor by two numbers. The ohms must not be lower than the manual states, or the chopper burns out. The watts depend on how often you brake.

Mount it outside the panel and away from cables because it gets hot, and wire its thermal switch to a drive input or to the contactor. Finally, enable the chopper in the parameters.

The result: a load that needed a 120 s decel without a resistor stops in 20 s with the right one.
