PWM and V/f

An IGBT is a switch: either on or off. How can a switch alone make a smooth sine wave at any frequency? The answer: PWM. And why, when we lower the frequency, do we have to lower the voltage with it? The answer: V/f. Both are in this simulator.

1PWM in a nutshell

The drive compares a reference wave (a sine at the frequency you want) with a very fast triangular carrier wave (thousands of times a second). When the reference is above the carrier the IGBT turns on, and when it is below it turns off. The result is pulses whose width grows and shrinks with the sine.

The motor windings (their inductance) smooth these pulses, so the current through them comes out almost a sine. For a lower frequency the reference gets slower. For a lower voltage the reference gets smaller, so the pulses get narrower.

3Why constant V/f?

The magnetic flux in the motor is proportional to voltage ÷ frequency. For the motor to stay strong (full torque) at any speed, this ratio has to stay constant:

V/f = 400 V ÷ 50 Hz = 8 V/Hz
SituationWhat happens
High voltage at low frequencyThe motor saturates: high current and heat
Low voltage at high frequencyWeak flux and less torque
Below ~10 HzWinding resistance eats part of the voltage ⇒ boost compensates
Above rated frequency (50 Hz)The voltage does not rise above the motor voltage ⇒ field weakening: higher speed with less torque
There is a quadratic V/f curve (variable torque) for pumps and fans: it gives less voltage at low speed because the load is light there, so it saves more.

4Carrier frequency

CarrierMotorDrive
Low (2–4 kHz)An audible whineRuns cooler, better with long cables
High (8–16 kHz)Quiet, with smoother currentRuns hotter ⇒ needs derating, and more EMC noise
From the field: an office next to the pump room complained about a whine. We raised the carrier from 4 to 8 kHz and the noise went away, but we checked the derating table in the manual to make sure the drive could still carry the motor current.
