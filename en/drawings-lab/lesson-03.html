Device tags, terminal numbers and cross-references

Symbols tell you what kind of device it is, but the numbers are what lead you to it in the panel. In this lesson you will learn why each device is named the way it is, why its terminals are numbered the way they are, and how to jump from a contact to its coil on another page with the cross-reference.

1The device name (device tag)

In IEC every device has a letter + a number with a dash in front. The letter tells you what the device does (IEC 81346-2), and the number is its sequence.

Note: many drawings call the contactor -K1 (or -KM1 in the older French system). The newer edition of IEC 81346-2 calls a power contactor -Q and an auxiliary relay -K. The idea is the same: read the legend page.

2Terminal numbers (terminal markings)

These numbers are printed on the device itself next to each screw, and the same number is written on the drawing. Once you understand them, you can find the terminal without searching.

TerminalsMeaning
1-2 · 3-4 · 5-6Main power contacts: odd numbers are inputs at the top, even numbers are outputs at the bottom
A1 - A2The coil
13-14 · 43-44Auxiliary contact NO: the second digits are 3-4
21-22 · 31-32Auxiliary contact NC: the second digits are 1-2
95-96 · 97-98Overload: 95-96 NC switches off the coil, and 97-98 NO is for a lamp or the PLC
15-16 · 15-18Timer contact: 16 opens and 18 closes after the time
X1 - X2The lamp
Decoding the number: 33-34 ⇒ the first digit (3) is the contact number, and the second digits (3-4) are its type = the third contact, NO. And 21-22 ⇒ the second contact, type NC.

When you see -K1:13 it means "terminal 13 on -K1", and -X2:6 means "terminal 6 on strip -X2".

3Wire numbers

Each single electrical point (equipotential) gets one number, and every wire connected to that point is marked with the same number on a ferrule at both ends. As soon as the wire passes through a device (a contact or a push button), the number changes.

  • Sequential numbering: 1, 2, 3... in order on the page. Simple and common.
  • Page and column numbering: like 3.4.1, meaning page 3, column 4, first wire, so the wire number takes you to the drawing.
  • Destination numbering: the ferrule shows the other end, like -K1:A1. You know where the wire goes while you are holding it.
Useful for troubleshooting: two terminals with the same wire number must have the same voltage. If one has 230V and the other 0V, the wire between them is broken.

4Cross-references: jump from a contact to its coil

The coil of -K1 may be on page 3, with its contacts spread across other pages. That is why:

  • Along the top of the frame are column numbers 1, 2, 3... and the reference /3.4 means page 3, column 4.
  • Under each contact is the location of its coil.
  • Under each coil is a small table (contact mirror) listing all its contacts: type, terminals and where each one is.

Try it: here are page 2 (power) and page 3 (control) for the same motor. Tap any blue reference.

Two pages and cross-references

In NEMA: the rungs are numbered on the left, and next to each coil on the right are the rung numbers where its contacts are, with NC contacts underlined. Same idea, different look.
