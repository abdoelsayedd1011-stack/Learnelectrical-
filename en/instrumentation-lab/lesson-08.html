Load cells and scales

Every scale in a plant, from filling scales to weighbridges and tank scales, has a load cell inside: a piece of metal that stretches a hair's breadth when loaded, with sensors bonded to it that feel that stretch. Here you will load a cell yourself and watch the bridge work, calibrate an indicator step by step like a technician, and pick up the multimeter to find the faulty cell.

1The strain gauge: a wire that stretches

A strain gauge is a very thin foil with a zig-zag metal track, bonded to the metal. When the metal stretches (tension) the track gets longer and thinner, so its resistance rises. When it is compressed, its resistance falls. The change is tiny: on a 350 Ω cell at full load the change is only about 0.7 Ω (0.2 %)! That is why we need a clever circuit to measure it: the bridge.

2The Wheatstone bridge and mV/V

The cell contains 4 gauges connected in a diamond (a Wheatstone bridge): two in tension and two in compression. We feed the bridge with a fixed voltage called the excitation (usually 5 or 10 V) at top and bottom, and measure the difference between the two side points (the signal). With no load the bridge is balanced and the difference is zero. With load a small difference in millivolts appears, linear with the load.

mV = Sensitivity (mV/V) × V exc × load ÷ capacity
Example: a 500 kg cell with a sensitivity of 2 mV/V on 10 V excitation ⇒ at 500 kg the output = 2 × 10 = 20 mV, at 250 kg = 10 mV, and at 1 kg only 0.04 mV (40 microvolts). That is why the indicator is a very sensitive instrument and noise is its enemy.

4The cable: 4-wire or 6-wire?

On 4-wire, the indicator supplies 10V but the cell receives a little less because the cable has resistance. Calibration removes that difference, but when the cable temperature changes (midday sun, winter night) its resistance changes and the reading drifts. On 6-wire there are two extra wires called sense that measure the voltage at the cell itself, so the indicator compensates for any drop. Try it on the bench above.

5Several cells and the junction box

A tank on 4 legs = 4 cells. They are all connected in parallel in a junction (summing) box: the excitation goes to all of them and the signals are combined, so the indicator sees the average of the cells as if they were one cell with 4 times the capacity.

  • Corner adjustment: put the same weight on each corner in turn, and it should read the same number. If one corner reads high, you adjust its potentiometer or resistor in the junction box (or digitally on modern indicators).
  • The junction box must be IP67, well sealed and with silica gel bags: moisture in it is the most common cause of a drifting reading.
  • The indicator shows the weight on its display, and gives 4–20 mA or Profinet/Modbus to the PLC, plus relays for filling set points.

6Calibration: zero and span with a known weight

Real calibration teaches the indicator two things: where zero is (the empty scale with its hopper or platform = the dead load) and how many kilos each mV is (using a known test weight). The general procedure (key names vary from indicator to indicator):

  1. Read the scale nameplate and enter the capacity, the division and the unit.
  2. Empty the scale completely, wait for the reading to settle, and do the ZERO calibration.
  3. Place certified test weights, ideally 50–100 % of capacity and at least 20 %, enter their exact value, and do the SPAN.
  4. Verify: remove the weight ⇒ must read 0. Put on a different weight ⇒ must read correctly. And check the corners.
  5. Record the result (as-left) and the weights' certificate number.
Zero is not tare: the calibration ZERO sets the basic zero point and is stored. The TARE key in normal use subtracts the empty container's weight temporarily so you see the net. And the zero key in normal use only clears dust or small residue (usually within ±2 %).

From the field: a technician did the span with a sugar sack marked "50 kg". The sack turned out to be 49.3 kg, so the whole scale read 1.4 % low: on a 10 tonne tank that is 140 kg out. Test weights must be certified and known.

Careful with the insulation tester: never test a load cell's insulation at 500V or 1000V: you will kill the gauges. If you use an insulation tester, use 50V only, with all the cell wires joined together and the test between them and the body. And before any welding on the scale structure: disconnect the cells or bypass them with a heavy earth cable next to each cell.

8Common field faults

Common field faults:
  • Zero drifts with the weather or after washing: moisture in the junction box, the cable or the cell.
  • The reading differs depending on which corner the load sits: corner adjustment, a weak cell, or something leaning on the structure.
  • The scale reads less than the truth: something is carrying part of the weight (force shunt): a rigidly fixed pipe, a ladder, tight cables, dirt between the platform and the frame. Pipes on a weighed tank must be flexible.
  • Side load: a cell measures in one direction. An angled load or friction spoils the reading and deforms the cell. Use proper mounting kits.
  • The scale failed after someone welded on the structure: the welding current passed through the cell and burnt it.
  • Overload from an impact: a weight dropped on the scale ⇒ the zero has shifted permanently.
Safety: the indicator is usually powered from 230V AC: switch off the power before undoing its terminals. And never go under a loaded tank or hopper hanging on load cells.
