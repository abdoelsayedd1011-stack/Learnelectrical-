Temperature 1: thermocouples

The thermocouple is the simplest temperature sensor there is: two wires of different metals welded together at one end. It handles up to 1700 °C, it's cheap and it's fast. But it has a "secret" that, if you don't understand it, will have you reading wrong for the rest of your career: the cold junction. In this lesson you will understand it and try it yourself on a bench with real tables.

1How does a thermocouple measure temperature?

A thermocouple is two wires of different metals welded at one end. When the hot end (the weld inside the furnace) is hotter than the cold end (the instrument terminals), a small voltage in millivolts appears between them.

So a thermocouple measures a temperature difference, not the temperature itself. The instrument must add the cold-end temperature, which is called CJC.

Example: a thermocouple gives 15.194 mV. Without CJC the instrument reads about 371 °C, which is wrong. When it adds 1.203 mV (panel at 30 °C) it becomes 16.397 mV, which is 400 °C and correct.

2What types of thermocouple are there?

K (up to 1250 °C) is the most common: furnaces, exhausts and plastics. J (up to 750 °C) is on older plastics machines. T (up to 350 °C) is for refrigeration and food.

N (up to 1250 °C) is more stable than K at high temperature. S and R (up to 1600 °C) are platinum, for glass and steel. B (up to 1800 °C) is for the highest temperatures.

4Why is compensating cable needed?

The point where the thermocouple meets copper is the cold end. So if you run copper wire from the sensor head, the cold end sits at the hot head and the reading is wrong.

There are 3 answers. Extension cable made of the same metals as the thermocouple (types K, J and T). Compensating cable, cheaper and good up to 100–200 °C (types S and R). Or a head-mounted transmitter giving 4–20 mA, the best answer because ordinary copper runs from there.

5How do you tell positive from negative?

Colours depend on the standard. In IEC the negative is white, and the positive depends on the type: K green, J black, T brown, N pink.

In ANSI the negative is red, and the positive is: K yellow, J white, T blue, N orange.

In ANSI red is negative. Not sure? Heat the tip: the mV must go up.

6The junction and the thermowell

The welded tip comes in 3 forms. Grounded: welded to the sheath, faster but may pick up noise. Ungrounded: insulated from the sheath, the safest. Exposed: bare, the fastest but it corrodes.

A thermowell is a pocket that protects the sensor and lets you change it without draining the line, but it slows the reading.

8Each wrong reading and its cause

Reads room temperature: the two wires are touching somewhere cold. Reading falls as the furnace heats: reversed polarity. A constant error of 20–40 degrees: the wrong type is set, there is copper wire, or CJC is off.

OPEN: the thermocouple is broken. Measure it in ohms: a good one is a few ohms and a broken one is OL. Reading jitters: a grounded type or the cable runs beside power cables. Type K drifting up at 800–1000 °C: this is green rot, replace it or use N.

Before pulling a sensor out of a thermowell on a steam line: make sure the well is not holed.
