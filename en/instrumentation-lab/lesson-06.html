Temperature 1: thermocouples

The thermocouple is the simplest temperature sensor there is: two wires of different metals welded together at one end. It handles up to 1700 °C, it's cheap and it's fast. But it has a "secret" that, if you don't understand it, will have you reading wrong for the rest of your career: the cold junction. In this lesson you will understand it and try it yourself on a bench with real tables.

1The idea: two metals and a temperature difference = millivolts

If you weld two wires of different metals (like nickel-chromium and nickel-aluminium) at one end and heat that end, a very small voltage (millivolts) appears between the other two ends. This is called the Seebeck effect.

  • Hot / measuring junction: the weld point inside the furnace.
  • Cold / reference junction: where the thermocouple wires end and connect to copper (the instrument terminals).
The most important sentence in this lesson: a thermocouple does not measure temperature. It measures the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions: mV = E(T hot) − E(T cold). So the instrument must know the cold junction temperature and add it. This is called CJC (cold junction compensation).
In numbers: Type K, furnace at 400 °C and panel at 30 °C. The table says E(400) = 16.397 mV and E(30) ≈ 1.203 mV ⇒ the meter reads 15.194 mV. Convert that straight from the table and you get about 371 °C (29 degrees out!). A proper instrument adds 1.203 ⇒ 16.397 mV ⇒ 400 °C.

4Compensating cable: why not copper?

The moment the thermocouple wire connects to copper, that point becomes the cold junction. Connect copper at the head and the cold junction is at the head (which could be 70 or 100 °C next to the furnace), while the instrument compensates for the panel temperature. The error = the difference between them.

  • Extension cable: exactly the same metals as the thermocouple (for K, J and T).
  • Compensating cable: a cheaper metal that gives the same mV at low temperature (up to about 100–200 °C), common with S and R because platinum is expensive.
  • Junction boxes and terminals along the way must be of the same type, or close together at the same temperature.
The best plant solution: a head-mount transmitter that converts the mV to 4–20 mA inside the head itself, with its CJC measuring the head temperature. After that, plain shielded copper cable to the panel and no problems at all.

A well-known trap: in IEC the negative is always white, and in ANSI the negative is always red. A technician used to "red is positive" will reverse an American thermocouple. Old cables to German (DIN) or British (BS) standards have different colours again. If unsure: warm the tip with your hand or with a lighter held at a distance, and the mV must rise positive.

6Junction types and thermowells

TypeHowPros / cons
GroundedThe weld point is welded to the metal sheathFastest response, but connected to earth so it can cause a ground loop and noise
UngroundedInsulated inside the sheath with powder (MgO)A little slower, but electrically isolated: the safest for measurement
ExposedThe weld point sticks out of the sheathFastest of all (air and gas), but corrodes quickly

Thermowell: a heavy metal pocket fitted into the pipe or tank, with the sensor inserted inside it. It protects the sensor from pressure, flow velocity and chemicals, and lets you change the sensor without draining the line. Its drawback is slower response: put a little thermal paste inside if allowed.

8Common field faults

Common field faults:
  • The instrument reads room temperature while the furnace is hot: the thermocouple is shorted (the two wires touching at a cool spot, creating a new hot junction there) or the instrument is reading another channel.
  • The reading falls while the furnace heats up: reversed polarity somewhere.
  • A fixed error of 20–40 degrees: wrong thermocouple type set in the instrument (J set as K), copper cable, or CJC turned off.
  • OPEN or full scale: the thermocouple is open (usually burnt out at the hot junction). Measure its resistance: a good one is a few ohms, an open one OL.
  • The reading is unstable: a grounded thermocouple near heaters or motors, or a cable next to power cables.
  • A Type K drifts upward after a while at 800–1000 °C: "green rot" and metallurgical change. Use Type N or a new sensor.
Safety: the thermocouple itself is millivolts and not dangerous, but the furnace and heaters next to it are 400V. And if you pull a sensor out of a thermowell on a steam line, make sure the thermowell is intact: if it is holed, steam will blow into your face.
