Encoders 2: absolute, mounting and faults

An incremental encoder counts, but if the power goes off it forgets where it is. An absolute encoder knows its position at any moment without counting. Here you will understand the Gray code inside it, the protocols, how to mount an encoder properly, and how to find its faults like a sharp maintenance technician.

1Incremental or absolute?

An incremental encoder outputs A B Z pulses and forgets its position when power is lost, so it needs homing. It connects to an HSC card and is the cheaper one. It suits motor speed and cut-to-length.

An absolute encoder gives a different number for every position, so it always knows where it is. It connects by SSI, Profinet or EtherCAT and costs more. It suits hoists, robots and presses.

Absolute comes in two kinds. Single-turn knows its position within one revolution: 12 bit means 4096 positions (0.088°). Multi-turn counts the revolutions too: 25 bit means 8192 positions × 4096 revolutions.

2Why does an absolute encoder use Gray code?

In plain binary, going from 7 to 8 (0111 → 1000) flips 4 bits at once. If they do not flip at exactly the same instant, strange numbers appear for a moment.

In Gray code the same step (0100 → 1100) flips one bit only, so there is no error.

Gray = Bin XOR (Bin >> 1)

4-bit absolute disc

On SSI, choose Gray or binary to match the encoder, or the reading jumps.

3How does an absolute encoder send its reading?

SSI: the card sends a clock and the encoder answers with the bits, and you must set the bit count and code type. BiSS-C: like SSI but faster and it checks the data with a CRC, used on servos.

Profinet / EtherCAT: the encoder is a device on the network with a GSDML or ESI file. CANopen / Profibus: older, found on cranes.

There is also the resolver: it outputs sin and cos signals with no electronics inside, so it withstands heat.

4How is homing done?

The axis moves slowly until it touches the reference limit switch, then carries on to the first Z pulse, and there the controller zeroes the counter.

The limit switch says "we are in this revolution", and Z fixes the exact point.

5How is an encoder mounted mechanically?

A solid shaft encoder is joined with a flexible coupling, because a rigid coupling wrecks the bearings. A hollow shaft encoder sits directly on the motor shaft with a flexible torque arm that stops it turning.

Never hit an encoder with a hammer: the glass disc inside breaks.

Draw a pen mark across the shaft and the coupling. If the two halves move apart, there is slip.

6Cable and noise

Encoder cable is twisted pairs: A with A̅ and B with B̅. The shield is usually earthed at both ends with a 360° clamp (per the maker's instructions). And the cable runs away from the VFD cable in a separate tray.

Cable length is set by the output type:

  • TTL 5V
    few m
  • RS-422
    ≈ 100 m

And 5 V on a long cable may reach the encoder as only 4.5 V.

7Each fault and its fix

Noise and electrical faults: extra counts with the VFD running, earth the shield and separate the cables. Counting backwards, swap A and B. Half the count or no direction, measure each channel alone. Counts lost at high speed, you need a faster card or less filtering.

Mechanical faults: a slow drift in the reading, the coupling or wheel is slipping. Noise and failed bearings, you need a flexible coupling or a torque arm.

The absolute reading jumps: the Gray/binary choice or the SSI bit count is wrong.

Game: find the encoder fault

Lock out before you touch the shaft. On a hoist the suspended load turns the motor: secure the load first.
