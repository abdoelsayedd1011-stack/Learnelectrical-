Encoders 1: incremental

The encoder tells the PLC, VFD or servo how far the shaft has turned, how fast, and in which direction. Without it a cut-to-length machine can't cut to size and a crane can't stop in the right place. In this lesson you will open up the encoder, turn it yourself, and see the pulses on a scope and count them.

1Where are encoders used?

  • Cut-to-length machine: a measuring wheel on the sheet metal or paper, and the encoder counts the distance until the shear comes down.
  • Conveyor: measuring speed and tracking a box's position so the air blast rejects it at the right moment.
  • Motor with a VFD (closed-loop vector): the drive reads the real speed and holds it steady even under load.
  • Cranes and tower cranes, presses, rotary tables: knowing the position to the millimetre.

2Inside: a disc, a light and a sensor

Inside an optical encoder there is a disc (glass or metal) with many equal slots, an LED on one side and photo sensors on the other. As the disc turns, the light passes through a slot and is blocked, so the sensor gives a pulse for every slot. The number of slots per turn is the PPR (pulses per revolution), such as 100, 1000, 1024 or 2500.

  • Channel A: one pulse per slot.
  • Channel B: exactly the same pulses, but its sensor is offset by a quarter of a slot, so it comes 90 electrical degrees behind or ahead of A (quadrature).
  • Channel Z (index / marker / reference): just one pulse per turn from a slot of its own. Used for the zero point (homing) and for counting turns.
Why do we need B at all? For direction. If A rises first and then B ⇒ turning one way. If B rises first ⇒ the other way. With one channel the PLC just counts and can't tell whether the shaft is going back or forward. B also lets us count 4 edges per slot (x4).

There are also magnetic encoders: a magnet and Hall sensors instead of the disc and light. They tolerate dust, oil and vibration better, but usually have lower resolution.

3Try it yourself: turn the encoder

This disc has only 16 slots so you can see them (a real one may have 1024). The sensors are fixed at the top: A and B on the outer ring and Z on the inner ring. Change the speed and direction, use "quarter-pulse step" to see the order of A and B slowly, and switch between x1, x2 and x4.

The PLC or high-speed counter follows this sequence: if it goes 10 → 11 → 01 → 00 it is CW and counts up, and if it goes the other way it is CCW and counts down. If it jumps two states at once (noise, or a speed beyond what the card can handle) that is an error and counts are lost.

Turns = counts ÷ (PPR × x)
Field example: a sheet-metal cutting machine: a measuring wheel with a 200 mm circumference on a 1000 PPR encoder with x4 counting ⇒ each count = 200 ÷ 4000 = 0.05 mm. Want to cut a 1.5 m sheet? The shear comes down after 1500 ÷ 0.05 = 30000 counts. And if the wheel wears and its circumference becomes 199 mm, every sheet comes out 0.5 % longer (7.5 mm in 1.5 m). That is why we calibrate the wheel length from time to time.
How to measure speed: either count pulses over a fixed time (frequency method, good at high speeds), or measure the time between two pulses (period method, better at very low speeds). VFDs and servos do both.

Very important: a 5V TTL encoder connected to 24V will burn. Read the label: some encoders are 4.75–30V with a TTL output, and some are 5V only.

6The frequency limit: can the card keep up?

f = PPR × rpm ÷ 60
Example: a 1000 PPR encoder on a 3000 rpm motor ⇒ 1000 × 3000 ÷ 60 = 50000 Hz = 50 kHz on each channel. With x4 that is 200 thousand counts per second.
  • An ordinary digital input on a PLC has a filter (3 ms, for example), so it manages a few hundred Hz at most. Never connect an encoder to an ordinary input.
  • You need a high-speed counter (HSC), a counter card or an encoder card in the VFD, and you must check that its maximum frequency (100 kHz or 200 kHz, for example) is above what you calculated.
  • The encoder itself has a maximum frequency (300 kHz, for example) and a maximum mechanical speed (6000 rpm, for example). A long cable lowers the maximum frequency, especially with HTL.

7From the field

Common field faults:
  • It counts down while the machine runs forward: A and B are swapped in the wiring. Swap them, or reverse the direction with a parameter in the card / VFD.
  • The count is right at low speed and lost at high speed: the frequency is above what the card can handle, or the digital filter is set high. Calculate f and check the HSC settings.
  • The count jumps by itself while the machine is stopped: noise (usually a cable next to the VFD motor cable), or the coupling vibrates with the encoder sitting on the edge of a slot. The next lesson has a game on these faults in detail.
  • It counts half: channel B is not connected and the x4 card counts only the A edges, or the card is set to x2 and you think it's x4.
