Encoders 1: incremental

The encoder tells the PLC, VFD or servo how far the shaft has turned, how fast, and in which direction. Without it a cut-to-length machine can't cut to size and a crane can't stop in the right place. In this lesson you will open up the encoder, turn it yourself, and see the pulses on a scope and count them.

1Where is an encoder used?

An encoder turns rotation into pulses that can be counted. In cut-to-length it counts the distance. On a conveyor it tells you the speed and where the can is. With a motor and VFD it keeps the speed steady under load. On a hoist or press it gives the position in millimetres.

2What does an encoder look like inside?

A disc with slots turns between an LED and a sensor. Each slot that passes gives a pulse. The number of slots per revolution is the PPR.

It outputs 3 channels. A gives a pulse per slot, and this is what we count. B is the same but 90° later, and from it we get the direction: A before B is CW, and B before A is CCW. Z is one pulse per revolution, the zero point.

There is also a magnetic encoder: it tolerates dust and oil better, but its resolution is lower.

3Try it: turn the encoder

The disc here has only 16 slots so you can see clearly. Use "quarter-pulse step" to watch the order of A and B slowly.

Encoder + scope + counter

The state of A and B goes in this order: 10, then 11, then 01, then 00. That order means CW and the counter goes up, and the reverse means CCW and it goes down. If the state jumps two steps at once, counts have been lost.

4What do x1, x2 and x4 mean?

The card can count one edge of A, both edges, or every edge of A and B. So the same 1000 PPR encoder gives different counts per revolution:

  • x1 A edge
    1000 · 0.36°
  • x2 both A edges
    2000 · 0.18°
  • x4 all edges
    4000 · 0.09°

Speed is worked out from the frequency of channel A:

rpm = f(A) ÷ PPR × 60

Example: a measuring wheel with a 200 mm circumference at x4 gives 0.05 mm per count. So a 1.5 m board equals 30000 counts.

If the wheel wears down to 199 mm, every board comes out 7.5 mm longer. Calibrate the wheel regularly.

5What output types do encoders have?

HTL (push-pull 10–30 V) is the most common with 24 V PLCs. TTL (line driver 5 V) outputs A and A̅ differentially, for high speeds and long cables. Open collector needs a pull-up resistor and is slow.

A 5 V TTL encoder on 24 V burns out. Read the label.

Wire colours differ between makers. With one maker the white wire is 0 V, and with another the white wire is channel B.

Colours have no standard: always go back to the datasheet.

6Will the card keep up?

Pulse frequency rises with speed and PPR:

f = PPR × rpm ÷ 60

So a 1000 PPR encoder at 3000 rpm gives 50 kHz per channel, and at x4 that is 200,000 counts per second.

An ordinary PLC input has a 3 ms filter, so it keeps up with a few hundred Hz only. That is why an encoder is wired to an HSC or an encoder card that handles 100–200 kHz.

7Problems from the field

Counts negative while moving forward: A and B are swapped, swap them or invert in the parameter. Counts half: channel B is not arriving, or the card is set to x2.

Loses counts at high speed only: the frequency is above what the card or the filter can take. Counts with the machine stopped: noise from the motor cable.
