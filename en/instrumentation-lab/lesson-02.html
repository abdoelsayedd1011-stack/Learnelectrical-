Analog signals: 4–20 mA and 0–10 V

Most transmitters in the world speak one language: a current from 4 to 20 milliamps. In this lesson you will understand why current and not voltage, why it starts at 4 and not zero, how to convert mA to a value, how to measure the loop properly, and how to tell the fault from the meter reading.

1Why is the signal a current, not a voltage?

In any series circuit the current is the same everywhere, so wire length does not change it. That is why a 4–20 mA signal travels hundreds of metres in the field without the reading being affected.

A 0–10 V signal is the opposite: a long wire loses part of the voltage, so the reading is wrong. We use it over short distances only.

2Why does it start at 4 and not zero?

This is called live zero. The lowest measured value gives 4 mA, so if the current falls far below that we know it is a fault, not a zero reading. Those 4 mA also power the transmitter itself.

The limits follow NAMUR NE43: 4 to 20 mA is normal measurement. 3.8 to 4 and 20 to 20.5 are slightly out of range but the signal is healthy. Below 3.6 mA is a fault: a broken wire or fail low. Above 21 mA is a fault: a short or fail high.

3A 2-wire, 3-wire or 4-wire transmitter?

The difference is where the power comes from. 2-wire is the most common: power and signal share the same two wires. 3-wire: a +24 V wire, a 0 V wire and a third for the signal. 4-wire: a separate supply and an active signal, as on a magnetic flowmeter.

2-wirepower and signal on the same pair. The most common.
3-wire+24 V, 0 V and a signal wire
4-wireseparate power + active signal (mag flowmeter)

In any loop only one side is active (the one that supplies power). Two active sides or two passive sides and the loop will not work.

4The 24 V supply and the 250 Ω resistor

The input card reads the current through a 250 Ω resistor: 4 mA gives 1 V across it, and 20 mA gives 5 V.

The supply must cover the transmitter (which needs about 10.5–12 V at its terminals, per the datasheet) plus the voltage dropped across the whole loop resistance:

V supply ≥ V min(TX) + 0.020 × R loop

So with a 24 V supply the maximum loop resistance is 675 Ω, which is comfortable. With a 12 V supply it is only 75 Ω, which is not enough.

5How do you convert mA to a value?

This formula gives the value from the current:

PV = LRV + (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × Span

And this is the reverse, the current from the value:

mA = 4 + (PV − LRV) ÷ Span × 16

Examples: a 0–10 bar pressure transmitter giving 12 mA means 5 bar. A −50 to 150 °C temperature transmitter giving 8 mA means 0 °C. A 0–4 m level transmitter at 3 m gives 16 mA.

7How do you measure loop current?

In order: put the loop in Manual. Set the meter to mA DC with the red probe in the mA socket. Open the loop and put the meter in series. Read and compare. Finally reconnect the wire and return the loop to Auto.

A meter on mA in parallel = a short and a wrong reading. In parallel you measure volts only.

8How is the cable installed?

Signal cable is twisted and shielded, because the twist and the shield stop noise. The shield is earthed at one end only, on the panel side. And the cable runs away from power cables in separate trays, crossing at 90° if they must cross.

9Each wrong reading and its cause

0 mA (wire break): a broken wire, a fuse, reversed polarity or no supply. 3.6 or above 21 mA: the transmitter itself is reporting a fault. A steady reading above 22 mA: a short, or water in the junction box.

Current stuck and not rising: a weak supply or too much loop resistance. Current jittering: a shield problem, a VFD cable nearby, or a loose terminal.
