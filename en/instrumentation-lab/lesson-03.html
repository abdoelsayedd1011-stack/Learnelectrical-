Digital sensors

Not every sensor measures a value. Most sensors on machines say just one thing: something there or not. Has the box arrived? Is the door closed? Has the cylinder finished its stroke? Here you will learn the types, finally understand PNP and NPN, and wire them yourself to a PLC card.

1Types of digital sensors

Limit switch

Mechanical: something hits the lever and it closes or opens a contact. Cheap and simple, but it wears out.

Inductive proximity

Creates a magnetic field and senses metals only, without contact. Short range (2–15 mm). The most common on machines.

Capacitive proximity

Senses almost anything: plastic, wood, powder, water behind a tank wall. It has a sensitivity screw you adjust.

Photoelectric

A beam of light (red or infrared). It senses anything that breaks or reflects the beam, and its range can be several metres.

Inductive sensing distance Sn: the figure in the datasheet is for mild steel. Stainless steel, aluminium and copper are sensed at a shorter distance (a correction factor like 0.7 or 0.4). A flush sensor (face level with the nut) has less range than a non-flush one (face sticking out) but can be embedded in metal. Try it on the bench below.

2NO and NC

NO (normally open) = the output is OFF while nothing is there, and switches on when it senses. NC (normally closed) = the output is ON while nothing is there, and switches off when it senses. On photoelectric sensors you will also hear light-on / dark-on.

Safety rule: anything that stops the machine (end-of-travel limits, protection) is made NC. Why? If the wire breaks, the PLC sees "OFF" and stops the machine instead of carrying on thinking all is well.

3PNP and NPN: who pushes and who pulls

3-wire electronic sensors have a transistor inside that acts like a switch on the black wire:

PNP = sourcing (switches the positive)
When it senses, the black wire goes to +24V. The sensor "pushes" current into the PLC. The card's COM is at 0V. This is the most common in Europe and much of the world.
NPN = sinking (pulls to 0V)
When it senses, the black wire is connected to 0V. The sensor "pulls" current from the PLC. The card's COM is at +24V. Common on Japanese and Chinese machines.

BrownBN+24V

The card and the confusing name: every manufacturer names the card its own way (some call the card that takes PNP a "sinking input"!). Don't rely on the name: look at where the card's COM goes. If it is at 0V ⇒ the card needs +24V on the input ⇒ PNP sensor. If it is at +24V ⇒ NPN sensor. Some cards have a COM that can be wired either way.

4The bench: wire the sensor to the PLC

Wire the sensor to the supply and card, then tap "Test wiring". I0.0 lights only when the target is close.

PNP and NPN bench

52-wire sensors and leakage current

Some sensors have only two wires (wired in series with the load, like a switch). To stay alive while "OFF" they must leak a small current (leakage current, about 0.5–1.5 mA), and when "ON" there is a voltage drop across them (about 5–8V).

From the field: a 2-wire sensor on a sensitive PLC card or a small relay: the input LED glows faintly all the time or the relay buzzes, even with no target. The cause is the leakage current. The fix: a bleeder resistor in parallel with the input to soak up the leakage (value and wattage per the datasheet), or switch to a 3-wire sensor.

6Common field faults

Common field faults:
  • A new sensor doesn't work at all with the PLC even though its LED lights: someone fitted an NPN in place of a PNP (or the other way round). The LED on the sensor lights because it sees the target, but the card doesn't understand it. Check the label before installing.
  • The sensor LED flashes: usually a shorted output (the black wire touching earth or 0V) or an overload.
  • The sensor detects sometimes and sometimes not: the target is at the limit of the sensing distance (the sensor was knocked back), or the material changed to aluminium. Mount it at about 70–80 % of Sn.
  • A retro-reflective sensor misses shiny cans: the can itself reflects the beam like the reflector. Use a polarized retro-reflective sensor.
  • Dust on the photoelectric lens or iron filings stuck to the proximity sensor ⇒ false signals. Cleaning is part of maintenance.
Safety: 24V DC is safe for you, but don't short +24 to 0V with the screwdriver while measuring: the supply will trip and the whole machine may stop. And any sensor on a guard door or a light curtain is part of the safety circuit: never bypass it.
