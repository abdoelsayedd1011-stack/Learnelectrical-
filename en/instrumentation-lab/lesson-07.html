Temperature 2: PT100, RTD and NTC

The PT100 is the king of accuracy in plants: a platinum resistance that rises with temperature in a precise, well-known way. More accurate and stable than a thermocouple up to about 500 °C. But it has an enemy called lead-wire resistance. Here you will see that error in numbers, understand why there are 2, 3 and 4 wires, and measure the sensor with a multimeter.

1What does PT100 mean?

An RTD is a sensor whose resistance changes with temperature. The PT100 is the most common: platinum, 100 Ω at 0 °C, rising 0.385 Ω per degree. The PT1000 is the same but 1000 Ω, so lead resistance affects it less.

T ≈ (R − 100) ÷ 0.385

2Accuracy: Class A and B

Sensors are graded by accuracy. This is the maximum error of each class at 100 °C:

  • AA
    ±0.27 °C @100
  • A
    ±0.35 °C @100
  • B (most common)
    ±0.80 °C @100

That is the sensor error only. The cable and the instrument have errors of their own.

3Why are there 2, 3 and 4 wires?

The instrument measures the sensor resistance together with the lead resistance. In a 2-wire connection, if each lead is 5 Ω the reading rises by 10 Ω, an error of 26 °C. So this is for short cables or a PT1000 only.

A 3-wire connection has an extra lead the instrument uses to measure the lead resistance and subtract it. This is the most common in plants. A 4-wire connection cancels the lead effect entirely and is used in calibration.

PT100 bench

Colours: red is one end and white is the other. Two wires of the same colour go to the same end.

4Self-heating and the head transmitter

To measure resistance the instrument passes a small current (about 1 mA) through the sensor. That current warms the sensor slightly, which is called self-heating. So if you measure with a meter that uses a high current, the reading rises.

Usually a transmitter is fitted in the sensor head to turn the resistance into 4–20 mA. For example, on a 0–150 °C range it gives 12 mA at 75 °C.

5How do you check a PT100 with a multimeter?

Disconnect the sensor first. Never measure ohms on something connected.

Between red and white it should read about 110–112 Ω at room temperature. Between two wires of the same colour you read lead resistance only (under 1–2 Ω). Between any wire and the sheath it must read OL.

To be surer: in ice it should read 100 Ω, and in boiling water 138.5 Ω.

6NTC and PTC

An NTC has resistance that falls as temperature rises. The most common is 10 kΩ at 25 °C (β 3950), found in air conditioners, fridges and heat sinks.

A PTC has resistance that jumps up at a set temperature. It is embedded in motor windings for protection: cold it is 100–750 Ω, and hot it reaches kΩ.

R(T) = R25 × e^( β × (1/T − 1/298.15) )

7Each wrong reading and its cause

Steadily above the truth: a 2-wire connection on a long cable, or the instrument set wrong. Rises with humidity: a corroding terminal or water in the head. Very slow: the sensor is not touching the bottom of the thermowell.

Under range: a short. Sensor break: a broken wire. Reads about −200 °C: a PT100 connected to an input set for PT1000.
