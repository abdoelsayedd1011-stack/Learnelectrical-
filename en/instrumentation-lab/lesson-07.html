Temperature 2: PT100, RTD and NTC

The PT100 is the king of accuracy in plants: a platinum resistance that rises with temperature in a precise, well-known way. More accurate and stable than a thermocouple up to about 500 °C. But it has an enemy called lead-wire resistance. Here you will see that error in numbers, understand why there are 2, 3 and 4 wires, and measure the sensor with a multimeter.

1What is an RTD? And a PT100?

RTD = resistance temperature detector: a sensor whose resistance changes with temperature. The best known is made of platinum (Pt):

  • PT100 = platinum with a resistance of 100 Ω at 0 °C.
  • PT1000 = platinum with a resistance of 1000 Ω at 0 °C (the change is 10 times bigger, so it is less affected by the leads).
  • The coefficient α = 0.00385 means the resistance rises by about 0.385 Ω per degree on a PT100.
R(T) = R0 × (1 + A·T + B·T²)

This is the Callendar–Van Dusen equation (above zero), with A = 3.9083×10⁻³ and B = −5.775×10⁻⁷. Below zero there is an extra term C. The instrument does this by itself; you just need to know the table:

The rule of thumb to keep in your head: T ≈ (R − 100) ÷ 0.385. Example: 119.4 Ω ⇒ 19.4 ÷ 0.385 ≈ 50 °C. This estimate drifts a little at high temperature (at 200 °C it gives 197), so use the table for accuracy.

32-wire, 3-wire and 4-wire

The instrument measures the resistance at its own terminals, so it measures the sensor + the leads. A 50 m copper cable of 0.5 mm² has, per wire:

R = 0.0175 × 50 ÷ 0.5 = 1.75 Ω
In numbers: if each wire is 5 Ω (a long, thin cable) in a 2-wire connection, the instrument reads 10 Ω extra. And 10 Ω on a PT100 = 10 ÷ 0.385 ≈ 26 °C of error! The sensor is at 100 °C and the display says 126.

Colours: per IEC 60751, one end of the sensor has a red wire and the other white. On 3-wire there are two wires of the same colour on the same end (red-red-white or red-white-white depending on the maker), and on 4-wire two red and two white. The key point: two wires of the same colour = the same end.

4Self-heating and the head transmitter

Self-heating: to measure resistance the instrument must pass a current through it (usually about 1 mA or less). That current warms the sensor a little (P = I² × R). In still air it can cause a noticeable error, so instruments use a very small current. Don't measure a working PT100 with an old meter that uses a high current and then wonder why the reading creeps up.

Head-mount transmitter: a small transmitter inside the sensor head that converts the resistance to 4–20 mA over a range you choose, for example 0–150 °C: at 0 ⇒ 4 mA, at 75 ⇒ 12 mA, at 150 ⇒ 20 mA. The cable to the panel is then plain copper and its resistance doesn't matter (lesson 2).

5Checking a PT100 with a multimeter

  1. Disconnect the sensor from the instrument or transmitter (never measure ohms on something connected).
  2. Meter on Ω. Measure between red and white: at room temperature (25–30 °C) you should find about 110–112 Ω.
  3. Measure between the two wires of the same colour: you should find only wire resistance (under 1–2 Ω).
  4. Measure between any wire and the sensor body (the sheath): it must be OL (insulated). If you find a value, there is moisture or leakage.
  5. Put the sensor in boiling water or melting ice: 138.5 Ω or 100 Ω (correcting for atmospheric pressure when boiling).

6NTC and PTC

These are thermistors, not platinum: cheap and very sensitive but non-linear.

  • NTC (negative): its resistance falls exponentially with temperature. The most common is 10 kΩ at 25 °C with β = 3950. Found in air conditioners, fridges, VFDs (heatsink temperature) and power supplies.
  • PTC (positive): low resistance up to a certain temperature, then it rises suddenly. Fitted inside motor windings (3 sensors in series) for thermal protection, connected to a protection relay or a VFD PTC input. Healthy and cold: about 100–750 Ω for all three; hot or open: many kilohms.
R(T) = R25 × e^( β × (1/T − 1/298.15) )

7Common field faults

Common field faults:
  • The reading is steadily higher than the truth: a PT100 wired 2-wire on a long cable, or the instrument set to 2-wire with a 3-wire sensor. Measure the lead resistance and calculate.
  • The reading rises over time in humid weather: a corroding terminal (contact resistance) or water in the head. On 3-wire this shows up as an error because it is in one wire only.
  • The instrument shows its minimum / under range: a short in the cable or the sensor.
  • The instrument shows its maximum / sensor break: the sensor or a lead is open.
  • The reading is very slow: the sensor is not touching the bottom of the thermowell, or the thermowell is too thick.
  • A PT100 wired to a PT1000 input on the instrument: it shows about −200 °C. Check the input type.
