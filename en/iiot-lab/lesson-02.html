Ethernet Fundamentals: IP, Subnet and Switch

Before any protocol can work, the signal has to get through. This lesson covers the cable, the switch and the addressing, which between them solve half of all network problems on the plant floor.

1What do the Ethernet port LEDs tell you?

The cable is Cat5e or Cat6 with an RJ45 or M12 connector, and its maximum length is 100 m. Near VFDs use shielded STP cable.

Look at the LEDs first. A steady green link means the cable is fine. No link means a broken cable, a dead port or a device with no power. Link with no activity means the device is connected but silent.

Don't run Ethernet alongside a VFD motor cable: the result is intermittent faults.

2What is the difference between a MAC and an IP address?

The MAC address, like 00:1C:06:…, is fixed at the factory and never changes. The IP address, like 192.168.1.10, is the one you set.

The /24 mask, 255.255.255.0, splits the address: the first 3 numbers are the "street" and the last is the "house". Two devices talk directly if they are on the same street.

Each device has four settings: IP is the device's own address. Mask decides who it sees directly. Gateway, like 192.168.1.1, is the way to anything outside its network. And DNS is needed for the cloud only.

3Static address or DHCP?

With DHCP the address may change at any time, so it is not allowed on a machine. The right way is a static address for every device in the panel, with a label on it.

It also helps to split the numbers by device type:

  • Routers
    1–9
  • PLCs
    10–49
  • HMIs
    50–99
  • VFDs and I/O
    100–199
  • Laptops
    200–250

4What are the four common faults?

A device comes and goes: a duplicate address, so unplug one of the two and ping. No ping but the LED is green: the two devices are on different subnets (1.x and 0.x), so match them.

Ping works but the software won't connect: a firewall is blocking port 502, 102 or 4840. And all devices disappear together: a cable loop caused a broadcast storm, and the cure is RSTP or MRP.

5How do you check the network with ping and arp?

Your first tool is ping, which asks a device: are you there?

ping 192.168.1.10

A Reply means the device is there and answering, so the network is not the problem. Timed out means a cable, an address or a powered-off device. Unreachable means your own PC is off the subnet, so check the mask.

The second tool is arp, which shows the MAC address behind each IP:

arp -a

One IP showing two MACs = a confirmed duplicate.
