Ethernet Fundamentals: IP, Subnet and Switch

Before any protocol can work, the signal has to get through. This lesson covers the cable, the switch and the addressing, which between them solve half of all network problems on the plant floor.

1Cable, port and LEDs

The Ethernet network in a control panel starts with a Cat5e or Cat6 cable and an RJ45 connector (or a sealed M12 in wet or high-vibration locations). The cable contains four twisted pairs; 100 Mbit/s uses two pairs and 1 Gbit/s uses all four. The maximum length of a single segment is 100 meters.

  • Link (steady green): the cable is sound and both ports have agreed on a speed. No link = a broken cable, a dead port or an unpowered device.
  • Activity (flashing yellow/green): data is flowing. Link without activity: the device is connected but not talking.
  • Shielded cable (STP/SFTP): essential next to drives and power cables. Bond the shield to ground at the panel end.
Warning: never route an Ethernet cable in the same duct as a drive motor cable. The interference causes intermittent errors that look like software faults.

2MAC address and IP address

Every network port has a fixed MAC address assigned at the factory (such as 00:1C:06:1A:2B:3C); the first three bytes identify the manufacturer. The IP address, by contrast, is one you assign, and it is what the PLC and the HMI work with.

An IPv4 address is four numbers from 0 to 255. The subnet mask defines which part of the address is the "street" and which part is the "house number". The mask 255.255.255.0 (written /24) means the first three numbers identify the network and the last one identifies the device. Devices from 192.168.1.1 to 192.168.1.254 are therefore on one network and talk to each other directly.

3Static or DHCP?

DHCP hands out addresses automatically from a router. It suits computers and phones, but it has no place on a machine network: if the router restarts and gives the PLC a different address, the HMI loses its connection. The rule: every device in the panel gets a static address, written on a label on the device and on the network drawing.

Good practice: reserve a range for each device type: 1–9 for routers and gateways, 10–49 for PLCs, 50–99 for HMIs, 100–199 for drives and I/O modules, 200–250 for temporary laptops.

5Your first tools: ping and arp

Open a command prompt on the laptop and type:

ping 192.168.1.10
  • Reply from … time<1ms: the device is present and responding. The problem is not the network.
  • Request timed out: no reply. The cable, the address, or the device is switched off.
  • Destination host unreachable: your own laptop is not on the correct network (usually the mask).
arp -a

Shows the table of addresses your laptop has seen, with their MAC addresses. If you see a single IP address with two different MAC addresses, you have a confirmed duplicate address.
