The encoder: the servo's eye

No encoder, no servo. In this lesson you will learn the difference between incremental and absolute with a power-loss experiment, calculate the resolution in PPR and bits, and understand why that huge resolution matters.

1Why is the encoder the most important part?

The drive uses the encoder for 3 things at the same time:

  1. Position: where the motor is (for the position loop).
  2. Speed: calculated from the change in position over time (for the speed loop).
  3. Magnet angle: to send current into the right winding (commutation).

That is why any problem with the encoder or its cable = the drive trips straight away with an alarm. You cannot fit an encoder without the drive "recognising" it, and on many models the encoder is aligned to the magnet angle at the factory, so do not remove the encoder from the motor.

2Incremental or absolute?

Incremental: outputs pulses A and B 90° apart (from which the drive knows the direction) and one Z pulse per revolution. The drive counts. If power is lost, the count is lost and a homing is needed. Resolution is given in PPR (pulses per revolution), and the drive counts all four edges: 2500 PPR × 4 = 10000 counts.
Absolute: every position in the revolution has its own code, so it knows its position as soon as power returns. A single-turn encoder knows the position within the revolution, and a multi-turn one counts the revolutions too (with a battery, internal gears or other battery-less methods). Resolution is given in bits: 17-bit = 131072 counts.

Try it: cut the power

From the field: a Battery low / Absolute data lost alarm on an absolute servo means the battery is weak. Replace it with the drive powered (not switched off) so the encoder does not lose the turn count, otherwise you will need to set the origin again. The exact method is in each brand's manual.

So why do we need that huge resolution if the machine accuracy is 0.01 mm? Because the drive calculates the speed from the position difference every fraction of a millisecond. At low speed, a low-resolution encoder gives "steps" in the speed reading, and the motor will hum and not run smoothly. High resolution = smoother motion and higher gain.

Do not confuse them: encoder resolution is not machine accuracy. Machine accuracy also depends on the ball screw (backlash and lead accuracy), the coupling and the installation.
