The encoder: the servo's eye

No encoder, no servo. In this lesson you will learn the difference between incremental and absolute with a power-loss experiment, calculate the resolution in PPR and bits, and understand why that huge resolution matters.

1Why is the encoder the most important part?

The encoder tells the drive 3 things: the position, the speed, and the magnet angle (commutation), without which the drive cannot turn the motor at all.

So any problem in the encoder or its cable raises an alarm. And never take the encoder off the motor.

2Incremental or absolute?

An incremental encoder outputs A, B and Z pulses, and the drive counts them. For example 2500×4 counts per revolution. Its drawback is that the count is lost when power fails, so you must home again.

An absolute encoder gives a different code for every position, for example 17-bit, so it knows where it is as soon as it powers up. The multi-turn type counts the revolutions too, usually with a battery.

Try it: cut the power

Battery low? Change the battery with the drive powered, or you will have to set zero again.

3Resolution: how many counts per revolution?

Resolution is the number of counts the encoder splits one revolution into. Each extra bit doubles it:

  • 2500×4
    10 000
  • 17-bit
    131 072
  • 20-bit
    1 048 576
  • 23-bit
    8 388 608

The higher the resolution, the smoother the drive can work out the speed, so motion is smoother and you can raise the gain.

Encoder resolution is not machine accuracy: the ball screw and coupling set that too.
