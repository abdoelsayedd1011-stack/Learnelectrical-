Homing and limits

The machine must know its zero, and it must not crash into the end. You will run a homing yourself on a ball screw axis, try the software limits and the limit switches, and see why limits are wired NC.

1Homing: the zero point

With an incremental encoder, when the drive starts up it does not know where the axis is. Homing means moving to a fixed mark on the machine and saying "zero is here".

  • Home sensor (home / dog): a proximity sensor or limit switch that marks the area roughly.
  • The Z pulse: after leaving the sensor, the drive looks for the first Z pulse from the encoder. Its position is exactly the same every time, so the zero is accurate to one pulse, not to the sensor's accuracy.
  • Other methods: homing on the limit switch, on a hard stop with a torque limit (stopper), or "current position = zero". Every drive or PLC has a numbered list of methods in its manual.

2Limits: 3 layers of protection

  1. Software limits: numbers in the drive or the PLC (for example from −50 to 280 mm). The first and gentlest protection, but they only work after homing.
  2. Limit switches (hardware): wired to inputs on the drive (forward / reverse inhibit) or the PLC. When one is hit, the drive blocks motion in that direction only.
  3. Hard stop: a mechanical stop at the end. If the axis reaches it, everything before it has failed.
Why are limits NC? If the wire breaks, an NC limit appears "hit" and the machine stops (safe). With NO, if the wire breaks nobody knows until the axis crashes.

Ball screw axis with sensors

With an absolute encoder: you set the zero (origin) once at installation, the drive remembers it even if power is lost, and the software limits work straight away. But if you replace the motor or undo the coupling, you must set the zero again.
