Electronic gearing

The PLC wants to say "move 250 mm", and the encoder counts hundreds of thousands per revolution. The electronic gear is what links them. You will learn the formula and calculate the gear for a ball screw, a belt and a rotary table with a gearbox.

1The problem: the PLC thinks in mm, the encoder in counts

A 17-bit encoder counts 131072 per revolution. If the PLC sent one pulse per count, then for the motor to turn at 3000 rpm it would need to output 131072 × 50 = 6.5 MHz, and no ordinary PLC can do that. Also, you want to say "move 250 mm", not "move 3276800 counts".

The solution: the electronic gear. The drive multiplies every incoming pulse by a fraction N / M and converts it to encoder counts. So you choose how many mm one pulse is.

2The formula in two steps

Pulses per rev = travel per motor rev ÷ pulse value
N / M = encoder counts per rev ÷ pulses per rev
Example: a ball screw with a 10 mm lead directly on the motor, a 10000 count encoder, and you want 1 pulse = 0.01 mm.
Pulses per rev = 10 ÷ 0.01 = 1000.
Gear = 10000 ÷ 1000 = 10/1. So each pulse = 10 counts = 0.01 mm. ✓
Travel per motor revolution is calculated from the mechanics:
  • Ball screw: the lead (pitch). If there is a gearbox or belt between the motor and the screw, divide by the ratio.
  • Pulley and belt: number of teeth × belt pitch (or π × diameter).
  • Rotary table with a 1:10 gearbox: one motor revolution = 360 ÷ 10 = 36°.
It differs from brand to brand: some drives write the gear as N/M (the numerator and denominator may have other parameter names), and some ask for the "number of pulses per revolution" directly and calculate it themselves. The idea is the same, but check in the manual which side is the numerator and which the denominator, and the allowed range of the fraction.

The trade-off: a smaller unit = higher resolution but a higher frequency. A larger unit = a lower frequency but each pulse is a bigger "jump". Choose the smallest unit whose frequency the PLC can output at maximum speed.
