Conductivity: conductors and insulators

Why is a wire copper on the inside and plastic on the outside? And why is water dangerous with electricity? You will try lots of materials on a tester, and work out wire resistance and voltage drop yourself.

1What is the difference between a conductor and an insulator?

A conductor has free electrons that can move, so current passes through it. That is the metals. An insulator has its electrons tied to their atoms, so current cannot pass. Examples are plastic, rubber and glass.

We measure how easily current passes with conductance, the opposite of resistance: G = 1 ÷ R, in siemens S.

2Which materials will you meet at work?

Conductors: copper in wires and windings, aluminium in overhead lines, silver on contactor contacts, and gold as a thin plating on connectors.

Insulators: PVC as the wire sheath, rubber in safety gloves and boots, ceramic and glass in pole insulators, and the air itself between high-voltage lines.

In between are the semiconductors such as silicon, which diodes and transistors are made from.

3Why is water dangerous with electricity?

Your body conducts electricity. A dry hand has high resistance, so little current passes. But a wet hand has much lower resistance, so the current from 220 V becomes enough to kill.

Never touch electricity when wet. Bathrooms and kitchens need a 30 mA RCD.

5Does the wire itself have resistance?

Yes. Even copper resists current a little, and the resistance of a wire is worked out like this:

R = ρ × L ÷ A

So the longer the wire (L) the higher its resistance, and the thicker it is (A) the lower. ρ is a number that depends on the metal: copper 0.0172 and aluminium 0.0282, so aluminium resists more. Here is the resistance of 100 m of wire:

  • Copper 2.5 mm²
    0.69 Ω
  • Alu 2.5 mm²
    1.13 Ω
  • Copper 1.5 mm²
    1.15 Ω

That resistance loses part of the voltage along the way, called voltage drop: ΔV = I × R. Note the length counts twice: out on the phase and back on the neutral.

6Does heat affect resistance?

The hotter a metal gets, the higher its resistance. Copper rises 0.4 % per degree. The clearest example is a 100 W filament lamp: cold it is 38 Ω, and when lit and hot it is 480 Ω.

That is why a multimeter reads a small number on a lamp that is off.

7Copper or aluminium?

Copper with a PVC sheath is the cable of homes and factories, because it is flexible and an excellent conductor. Aluminium is used on overhead lines, because it is a third of the weight and cheaper.

Never join copper to aluminium directly: it corrodes, heats up and catches fire. Use a bi-metal connector.

8How are voltage, current and resistance related?

The three are tied together: more voltage gives more current, and more resistance gives less current. Try it yourself:

Ohm’s law experiment

This relation is Ohm's law: current = voltage ÷ resistance.
