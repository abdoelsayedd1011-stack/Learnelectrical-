Conductivity: conductors and insulators

Why is a wire copper on the inside and plastic on the outside? And why is water dangerous with electricity? You will try lots of materials on a tester, and work out wire resistance and voltage drop yourself.

‹ Lessons← All courses

1Why do some things conduct and others don’t?

In metals, the outermost electrons of each atom are only loosely held and move easily from atom to atom. They are called free electrons. As soon as you apply a voltage, they all move together ⇒ current.

In insulators (plastic, rubber, glass), the electrons are tightly bound to their atoms and don’t move, even with a normal voltage applied. So there is no current.

Conductance is how easily a material lets current through. It is the opposite of resistance: G = 1 ÷ R, and its unit is the siemens (S). A wire of 0.5Ω has a conductance of 2S.

3Water and the human body

Very pure (distilled) water is a poor insulator. But tap water, sweat and water on the floor have dissolved salts and minerals, and those salts make it conduct. The more salt, the better it conducts.

The human body is mostly water and salts, so it is a conductor. Dry skin protects you a little (it has high resistance), but with wet or sweaty hands the resistance drops sharply, and the current that flows from 220V is enough to kill.

That is why: never touch anything electrical with wet hands or feet, bathrooms and kitchens need a 30mA RCD, sockets go away from the sink, and you never switch on an appliance that has fallen into water.

4Try it: the material tester

A 3V battery, a small lamp and two clips. Put the material between the clips: if it is a good conductor the lamp lights, and the ohmmeter measures the sample’s resistance. Try metals, insulators, water and a hand.

5Wire resistance: length, thickness and material

R = ρ × L ÷ A
  • ρ (rho) = the material’s resistivity: copper 0.0172, aluminium 0.0282 (in Ω·mm²/m).
  • L = wire length in metres: longer ⇒ more resistance (the electrons travel further).
  • A = cross-section in mm²: thicker ⇒ less resistance (a wider path, like a wider pipe).
Example: a 2.5mm² copper wire 100 metres long ⇒ R = 0.0172 × 100 ÷ 2.5 = 0.69Ω. The same length in 1.5mm² ⇒ 1.15Ω. And 2.5mm² aluminium ⇒ 1.13Ω.

That resistance is small, but when a large current flows through it, voltage is lost (voltage drop ΔV = I × R) and heat is produced. And remember the current goes out on the live and comes back on the neutral, so it is twice the length.

6Heat changes resistance

In metals, the hotter the wire, the more its atoms vibrate and get in the electrons’ way ⇒ resistance goes up. Copper rises about 0.4% per degree: a wire at 70°C has about 20% more resistance than at 20°C.

An example from work: a 100W filament lamp is about 38Ω cold and about 480Ω when lit (the filament is at 2500°C). That is why you get a small number when you measure it with a multimeter, and that is normal (you will see it in lesson 9). It is also why filament lamps usually blow at the moment you switch them on.

7Why copper inside PVC? And why aluminium on poles?

House and factory cables: copper + PVC. Copper is an excellent conductor, flexible, doesn’t break when bent, and is easy to clamp under terminal screws. PVC is a cheap, flexible insulator that withstands up to 70°C, so it stops you touching the copper and prevents shorts between wires.
Overhead lines: aluminium. Aluminium weighs about a third of copper and is much cheaper. It does need about 1.6 times the cross-section, but it is still lighter and cheaper. It is wound around a steel core that takes the pull between the poles, and the air around it is the insulator.
Never join copper directly to aluminium: the contact point corrodes, so its resistance rises, it heats up, and a fire can start. Use the special bi-metal connectors.
