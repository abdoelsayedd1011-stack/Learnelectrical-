Series and parallel connections

You have more than one lamp or appliance: do you connect them one after another or side by side? That difference is why each lamp in a house works on its own, while old string lights all go out when a single bulb fails.

1Series: one single path

In series, the devices are one after another on a single path. The current has no other way to go, so it flows through all of them.

  • The current is the same in every device: I = I1 = I2 = I3
  • The voltage is shared between them, and adds up to the source voltage: V = V1 + V2 + V3
  • Total resistance = the sum: R = R1 + R2 + R3 (bigger than the biggest one)
  • The device with the higher resistance gets more voltage (V1 = I × R1).
  • If one burns out or comes loose ⇒ the circuit is broken ⇒ everything goes off.
Example: 3 lamps of 12Ω in series on 12V ⇒ R = 36Ω, I = 12 ÷ 36 = 0.33A, and each lamp gets only 4V, so they glow dimly. The same thing happens in an old string of lights: 50 small bulbs of about 4.4V each on 220V, and when one bulb blows the whole string goes out.

2Parallel: each one has its own path

In parallel, each device is connected on its own between the two terminals (in a house: between live and neutral).

  • The voltage is the same across every device: they all get the full 220V.
  • The currents add up: I = I1 + I2 + I3. Every extra appliance you switch on increases the current in the main wire.
  • Total resistance: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3, and for just two: R = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2). Always smaller than the smallest one.
  • If one burns out ⇒ the rest keep working normally.
A house is all parallel: every lamp and every socket is connected to live and neutral on its own, so each appliance gets 220V and switches on and off independently. But be careful: when you run a water heater + a kettle + an iron on the same circuit, the currents add up in the cable and the breaker (9 + 10 + 5.5 = 24.5A) and a 16A breaker will trip.

You will wire two lamps in parallel yourself on the wiring board in lesson 12.

4Batteries: series or parallel?

Series (positive to negative): the voltages add up and the capacity stays the same. A remote with two 1.5V batteries gives 3V. A torch with 4 gives 6V. A UPS with two 12V batteries gives 24V.
Parallel (positive to positive): the voltage stays the same and the capacity (amp-hours, Ah) adds up. Two 12V 100Ah batteries in parallel give 12V 200Ah: the same voltage, but they last twice as long.
Be careful: don’t connect batteries in parallel if their voltages differ, or a new battery with an old one: the higher one discharges into the lower one with a large current and heats up. And if one battery is put in backwards in series, its voltage subtracts instead of adding.

5A mixed circuit: one example step by step

R1 = 4Ω in series with (R2 = 12Ω and R3 = 6Ω in parallel), and the source is 12V:

  1. The parallel part first: R23 = (12 × 6) ÷ (12 + 6) = 4Ω
  2. The total: R = 4 + 4 = 8Ω
  3. The total current: I = 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5A (this is the current in R1)
  4. The voltage across R1: 1.5 × 4 = 6V, and the rest is across the parallel part: 12 − 6 = 6V
  5. Current in R2: 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5A, and in R3: 6 ÷ 6 = 1A (together 1.5A ✓)

Try another example in "Mixed" mode above: L1 = 6Ω and L2 = L3 = 12Ω on 12V. The parallel part = 6Ω, total = 12Ω, current = 1A, L1 gets 6V, and L2 and L3 each get 6V and 0.5A. See if the numbers in the table match your working.
