What is electricity?

Before you pick up a screwdriver or a multimeter, you only need to understand 4 words: voltage, current, resistance and power. We will explain them with water in pipes, and then work out an electricity bill ourselves.

1So what is electricity?

Inside every copper wire there are tiny particles called electrons. When we connect the wire to a battery or to the mains, these electrons move along the wire around a closed loop. That movement is electric current.

The easiest way to understand it: imagine the wire is a pipe and electricity is water flowing through it.

ElectricityMeasured inLike what in water
Voltagevolt VThe pressure that pushes the water (a higher tank gives more pressure)
Currentampere AThe amount of water passing through the pipe every second
Resistanceohm ΩA narrowing in the pipe that reduces the water getting through
Powerwatt WThe work the water does (like turning a turbine)
The golden rule: voltage pushes, resistance holds back, and current is the result. So more voltage ⇒ more current, and more resistance ⇒ less current.

+Inside the atom: free electrons

Everything around us is made of tiny atoms. At the centre of the atom is a positive (+) nucleus, and around it negative (−) electrons orbit.

In metals like copper, the outermost electron of each atom is only loosely held, so it moves easily between atoms. It is called a free electron. A wire is full of countless free electrons moving at random. As soon as we connect a battery, the voltage pushes them all in one direction: that is the current.

2Try it yourself: water and electricity

Move the voltage and resistance sliders and watch both at once: the tank and pipe on top, the battery and lamp below. Notice the speed of the flow and the brightness of the lamp.

Ohm’s law experiment

I = V ÷ R

This is Ohm’s law: current (amps) = voltage (volts) ÷ resistance (ohms). You will see it at work in lesson 4 (series and parallel), and use it to choose the cable and breaker in lesson 11.

3Power (watts): how much does this appliance use?

Every appliance has its power in watts (W) written on the nameplate (the sticker with its ratings). Power = voltage × current:

P = V × I

Household mains here is 220 volts, so you can work out the current an appliance draws: I = P ÷ 220.

Example: a 2000W water heater on 220V ⇒ I = 2000 ÷ 220 = 9.1A (9.1 amps). So the heater alone draws as much current as 180 LED lamps! That is why appliances that heat need a thicker cable and their own breaker.

You will study power in detail in lesson 5. 1 kilowatt (kW) = 1000 watts. A mechanical horsepower (HP) is about 746 watts, but a "1.5 HP" air conditioner is a cooling size; its real electrical power is written on its nameplate.

4The electricity bill: the kilowatt-hour (kWh)

The meter at your front door doesn’t count watts, it counts energy: power × time. The unit is the kilowatt-hour, kWh (people often call it a "unit").

kWh = (W ÷ 1000) × hours
Example: a 1400W air conditioner running 8 hours ⇒ 1.4 × 8 = 11.2 kWh a day, which is about 336 kWh a month. A 10W LED lamp running the same time ⇒ only 0.08 kWh a day!

Change the hours in the table and see your monthly use. The price depends on your tariff band, so enter the price per kWh from your own bill.

Work out your home’s consumption

Saving tip: appliances that heat (water heater, kettle, iron, electric heater) and the air conditioner are what make the bill big. Lamps and chargers hardly make a difference.
