Power and energy

How much does this appliance "eat"? And why does the water heater push the bill up while a lamp doesn’t? We will understand the watt and the kilowatt-hour, work out the bill for a whole home, and see why a wire heats up and a fuse blows.

1Power: how fast energy is used

Power is the rate at which energy is used (or the rate of doing work). Its unit is the watt, W: 1 watt = 1 joule of energy every second. A 2000W water heater turns 2000 joules of electricity into heat every second, while a 10W lamp turns only 10 joules.

P = V × I

So power = voltage (the push) × current (the amount). Higher voltage or more current ⇒ more power. And if we combine this with Ohm’s law (V = I × R) we get two more formulas:

P = I² × R
When you know the current and resistance. Useful for working out the heat in a wire.
P = V² ÷ R
When you know the voltage and resistance. A 24.2Ω heater on 220V ⇒ 220 × 220 ÷ 24.2 = 2000W.
Notice: from P = V² ÷ R: if the voltage drops to half, the power drops to a quarter! A 2000W heater on 110V gives only 500W. That is why appliances run so weakly when the mains voltage is "low".

Horsepower on a motor means the mechanical power coming out of the shaft. The motor draws a bit more electricity than that, because part of it is lost as heat (efficiency, below). As for a "1.5 HP air conditioner", that is a cooling size, and its real electrical power (about 1.1–1.5 kW) is written on its nameplate.

An example from the factory: a 7.5 kW motor is marked 10HP. 7.5 ÷ 0.746 ≈ 10 HP ✓.

4Energy: power × time

Power tells you "how fast", and energy tells you "how much in total". A 2000W heater running for a minute uses far less than the same heater running for two hours.

E = P × t
kWh = (W ÷ 1000) × h

That is: kilowatt-hours = (watts ÷ 1000) × number of hours (h).

  • Watt-hour, Wh: a 1 watt appliance running for an hour. A power bank might be marked 37Wh, for example.
  • Kilowatt-hour, kWh (often just called a "unit"): this is what the meter counts and what you pay for. 1 kWh = a 1000 watt appliance running for an hour, or a 10 watt lamp running for 100 hours.
A bill example: 6 LED lamps of 10W running 6 hours: 0.06 kW × 6h = 0.36 kWh. A fridge about 1.5 kWh. A 1400W air conditioner running 8 hours: 1.4 × 8 = 11.2 kWh. A 2000W water heater for an hour: 2 kWh. The total is about 15 kWh a day ≈ 450 kWh a month. Notice that the air conditioner alone is about two thirds of the bill.

Try it for your home: tick the appliances you have, change the watts, quantities and hours, and enter the price per kWh from your bill.

Real bills are in tariff bands: the more you use, the higher the price per kWh, so the figure here is approximate. A fridge may be marked 150W, but its compressor doesn’t run 24 hours, so we put about 10 hours.

5Heat: why a wire gets hot and a fuse blows

Any current flowing through a resistance produces heat: P = I² × R. Notice the current is squared: if the current doubles, the heat becomes 4 times as much.

  • Water heaters, irons and kettles are made to heat up: a nichrome element with high resistance.
  • A wire has a small resistance, so it only warms a little. If you load it with more current than its size allows, it gets very hot and melts the insulation ⇒ a short circuit and a fire. (That is why we choose the cable and breaker correctly in lesson 11.)
  • A fuse is a thin wire designed to heat up, melt and break the circuit if the current goes above its rating, protecting what comes after it.
  • A loose connection (a screw not tightened, a loose plug) has a higher resistance, so it heats up and blackens the socket. This causes many fires.
  • An extension cable left coiled on its reel while running a heater: the heat can’t escape, so it melts inside.
Work it out: a 2.5mm² wire 20 metres long, out and back, has a resistance of about 0.28Ω. With a 9A heater ⇒ 9² × 0.28 ≈ 23W of heat lost in the wire (normal, spread over 40 metres). The same wire at 30A ⇒ ≈ 250W: that wire will catch fire.

6Efficiency: how much of the electricity does the useful work

η = Pout ÷ Pin × 100%

Efficiency (η) = the useful power out ÷ the electrical power in. The rest is lost, usually as heat.

ApplianceEfficiency (approx.)Where the rest goes
60W filament lamp≈ 5%Heat (the bulb burns your fingers)
9W LED lamp (same light)≈ 30–40%A little heat
Small motor (a fan)≈ 50–70%Heat in the windings and friction
Large factory motor≈ 85–95%Heat
Water heater≈ 100% heatIt is made for heat in the first place
Example: a 9W LED lamp gives the same light as a 60W filament lamp ⇒ it saves 51W. If it runs 6 hours a day ⇒ 0.051 × 6 × 30 ≈ 9 kWh a month for that one lamp. And a motor giving out 1.5kW at 85% efficiency ⇒ draws 1.5 ÷ 0.85 ≈ 1.76 kW from the supply.
