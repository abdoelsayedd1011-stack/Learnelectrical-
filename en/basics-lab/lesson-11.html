Ohm’s law, power and choosing the cable and breaker

From the power written on an appliance you will work out the current, and from that choose the right breaker and a cable size that can carry it without heating up.

‹ Lessons← All courses

1Which two formulas will you need every day?

The first gives current from power: I = P ÷ V. You use it every day to find how many amps an appliance will draw.

The second is Ohm's law: V = I × R. Know two and you get the third.

3Example: what wire and breaker does a 3000 W heater need?

First work out the current: 3000 ÷ 220 = 13.6 A. Then pick the first breaker above that current: 16 A. Finally pick the wire that can carry that breaker: 2.5 mm².

The rule: the wire is sized to the breaker, not to the appliance, because the breaker is what protects the wire.

A 25 A breaker on 1.5 mm² wire = the wire melts before the breaker trips.

A long run or continuous duty? Go up one step: 4 mm² and a 20 A breaker.

4Which breaker goes with each wire?

1.5 mm² with 10 A for lighting. 2.5 mm² with 16 A for sockets and a heater up to 3000 W. 4 mm² with 20–25 A for a heater, air conditioner or oven.

6 mm² with 32 A for an electric cooker and feeder lines. 10 mm² with 40 A for the main feed of a flat.

Breaker sizes: 6 · 10 · 16 · 20 · 25 · 32 · 40 A. Curve C for motors and air conditioners.

This is an approximate table. Follow your local code and an engineer for large loads.
