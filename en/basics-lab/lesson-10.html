The transformer and the power supply

Control panels and chargers don’t run on 220V directly. The transformer steps the voltage down (to 24V or 12V, for example), and the power supply turns AC into steady DC. You will understand both, measure them and learn their faults.

1How does a transformer work?

A transformer is an iron core with two coils on it that are not connected to each other at all:

  • The primary: the 220V goes into it. The AC in it makes a changing magnetic field in the core.
  • The secondary: that magnetic field induces a voltage in it, and the voltage depends on its number of turns.
Vs ÷ Vp = N2 ÷ N1
Example: the primary has 1100 turns and the secondary 120 turns, with 220V in ⇒ Vs = 220 × 120 ÷ 1100 = 24V.
Important: a transformer works on AC only. If you connect it to DC it gives nothing out, and the primary draws a large current and burns out.

3The VA rating and the current on each side

A transformer is marked with its power in VA (volt-amperes). The power going in is about equal to the power coming out (transformers are very efficient), so when the voltage goes down the current goes up:

A 100VA, 230/24V transformer:
Secondary current = 100 ÷ 24 = 4.2A (full load).
Primary current = 100 ÷ 230 = 0.43A.
So the secondary wire must be thicker, and the fuse on the 24V side should be about 4A, not 0.5A.

Isolation: a safety feature

No wire connects the primary to the secondary; the energy passes only through magnetism. That is why the output of a control transformer is isolated from the mains, and a 24V control circuit is much safer if someone touches it. In many panels the 0V terminal of the secondary is connected to earth, so that any short to the enclosure blows the fuse.

4Testing a transformer with a multimeter

  1. With it disconnected: measure the resistance of the primary and the secondary. The coil with the higher resistance is the high-voltage side.
  2. Insulation: OL between primary and secondary, and OL between either coil and the core.
  3. With it powered: measure the output on V~.

5The power supply: from AC to DC

A PLC, sensors, small relays and a phone charger all need steady DC. A power supply does this in stages. Press each stage and watch the waveform change:

Power supply stages

Switch-mode (SMPS): the power supplies in panels and phone chargers are the "switching" type. Instead of a big heavy transformer, they rectify the 220V straight away, chop it very fast (tens of thousands of times a second) through a small transformer, then rectify and smooth it again. The result: light, small and efficient, and it usually runs on anything from 100 to 240V.

6Measuring a power supply and its faults

A power supply is measured while it is powered (volts only): the input on V~ between L and N, and the output on V⎓, red on +V and black on −V.
